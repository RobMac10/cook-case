Case 2 · Year 9 · Read

Did he have the consent of the natives?

Draft, pending consultation with Gweagal and Dharawal communities of Kamay, including families at La Perouse, and with AIATSIS guidance. This case describes a landing in which muskets were fired and a man was struck by small shot. The two Gweagal men who opposed the landing are not named in the 1770 journals. Some later accounts name one man Cooman; that identification is disputed. Their own words were not written down by them. No likeness of them has been invented.

What is agreed

  • On 30 July 1768 the Admiralty issued additional (“secret”) instructions to Lieutenant James Cook. They told him: “You are also with the Consent of the Natives to take Possession of Convenient Situations in the Country in the Name of the King of Great Britain: Or: if you find the Country uninhabited take Possession for his Majesty by setting up Proper Marks and Inscriptions, as first discoverers and possessors.”
  • The Endeavour entered Kamay (Botany Bay) in late April 1770. Cook’s journal dates the landing Sunday, 29 April. Joseph Banks records the same landing under 28 April. Historians treat these as the same event; the dating difference is flagged, not hidden.
  • Cook, Banks, Dr Solander and the Ra‘iatean navigator Tupaia (written “Tupia” in the journals) went in toward the south shore. Most people moved away. Two men came down and opposed the landing. After an exchange that included musket fire, a stone thrown, small shot that struck one man, and darts or lances thrown at the visitors, the two men went off. The landing party went to the houses, left beads and other things, and took away spears (Banks: forty or fifty lances).
  • The journals do not record a grant of consent. The next day Cook wrote that presents could not entice people to the officer on shore: “all they seem’d to want was for us to be gone.”
  • Cook did not perform a formal taking of possession at Kamay. On 22 August 1770, at what he called Possession Island (Bedanug / Tuidin, in the Torres Strait), he recorded hoisting colours and taking possession of the eastern coast in the name of King George III. That later act is in Super Sleuths. This case’s question is about consent.

Ask three witnesses

Each card uses recorded words. The third card is about the Gweagal men who opposed the landing. It uses what Cook, Banks and Parkinson wrote, and it says plainly what was not written down. Ask the same three questions of each witness.

James Cook

Lieutenant, commander of HMB Endeavour · journal, 29–30 April 1770

His own journal of the landing, and of the next day.

Source. Cook’s journal, Sunday 29 April and 30 April 1770. A widely used transcription is quoted by the State Library of NSW (“Violence on the shore”; “Eight days in Kamay”) from Safe 1/71. Wording here follows that published journal text.

What they said. On 29 April he wrote that, as the boats approached, “they all made off, except 2 Men, who seem’d resolved to oppose our landing.” He could not understand their words, “for neither us nor Tupia could understand one word they said.” He threw nails and beads ashore, “which they took up, and seem’d not ill pleased with,” so that he “thought that they beckon’d to us to come ashore; but in this we were mistaken, for as soon as we put the boat in they again came to oppose us, upon which I fir’d a musquet between the 2.” One man “took up a stone and threw at us, which caused my firing a Second Musquet, load with small Shott; and altho’ some of the shott struck the man, yet it had no other effect than making him lay hold on a Target.” After they landed, “they throw’d 2 darts at us,” and he fired a third shot. On 30 April: “Mr. Hicks, who was the Officer ashore, did all in his power to intice them to him by offering them presents; but it was to no purpose, all they seem’d to want was for us to be gone.”

Could they really have seen it? He was in the boats and on the shore. He was also the officer who ordered the shots, and the man writing the official journal. He could not understand the language. He first thought a gesture was an invitation, then wrote that he had been mistaken.

Why might they say it? To record a landing for the Admiralty, and to explain the use of muskets. A commander’s journal is evidence of what he said happened. It is not a neutral film of the beach, and it is not the Gweagal men’s account.

Joseph Banks

Naturalist on the Endeavour · journal, 28 April 1770 (his dating of the landing)

A second journal of the same afternoon, written by a man in the boats who was not in command.

Source. The Endeavour Journal of Sir Joseph Banks, entry beginning “1770 April 28,” including the landing after dinner. Wikisource transcription of the journal: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Endeavour_Journal_of_Sir_Joseph_Banks/April_1770 The original is held by the State Library of NSW (Banks Papers, Series 03.02).

What they said. Banks wrote that as the boats approached the rocks, “two of the men came down upon them, each armd with a lance of about 10 feet long.” “They calld to us very loud in a harsh sounding Language of which neither us or Tupia understood a word, shaking their lances and menacing, in all appearance resolvd to dispute our landing to the utmost tho they were but two and we 30 or 40 at least.” He recorded them “waving to us to be gone,” while the visitors signed that they wanted water and “meant them no harm.” “They remaind resolute so a musquet was fird over them.” After small shot struck the elder man on the legs, he “ran up to the house… and soon returnd with a sheild.” Lances were thrown; more small shot was fired; the two men ran away. Banks then wrote: “We however thought it no improper measure to take away with us all the lances which we could find about the houses, amounting in number to forty or fifty.”

Could they really have seen it? He was there, close enough to describe paint, weapons, and distances (he says the elder man was about forty yards from the boat). He also wanted plants, and he helped decide to take the lances. He dated the landing 28 April; Cook dated it 29 April.

Why might they say it? To keep a private scientific journal of what he saw, including the taking of the spears — which he felt the need to justify as “no improper measure.” That phrase is evidence of a choice, not of permission.

The Gweagal men at Kamay

Two men who opposed the landing, 29 April 1770 · their own words were not written down by them

This card does not invent a speech. It sets out what the visiting journals said the two men did, and one phrase a shipboard artist later published as their call.

Source. Cook’s journal, 29–30 April 1770 (as above). Banks’s journal, 28 April 1770 (as above). Sydney Parkinson, A Journal of a Voyage to the South Seas (1773), records that the two men “often crying to us, Warra warra wai.” Senior Gweagal knowledge holder Dr Shayne Williams has explained that phrase, in later published comments used by the State Library of NSW, as meaning “you are all dead” — an observation, in his account, rather than a simple threat. The 1770 journals do not name either man. Some later oral histories, including accounts linked to Biddy Giles and to descendant Rodney Kelly, name one of the men Cooman. Other local voices, including comments reported from the Gujaga Foundation, have said there is no single agreed identification. Maria Nugent and Gaye Sculthorpe have written that there is “no consensus” that Cooman was one of the two. This file does not choose a name and then pretend the journals used it.

What they said. We do not have a statement in their own hand. What was written down is action, and one published call. Cook: they “seem’d resolved to oppose our landing,” came again to oppose the boat, threw a stone, threw “2 darts,” and, the next day, “all they seem’d to want was for us to be gone.” Banks: they called out, shook their lances, were “waving to us to be gone,” remained “resolute,” and threw lances after the visitors had landed. Parkinson, who was on the voyage, published the words “Warra warra wai.” The meaning of that phrase is given here from later Gweagal explanation, not as if Cook had translated it in 1770.

Could they really have seen it? They were on their own Country, on the rock and the beach, at close range. The problem is not their presence. The problem is the record: everything on this card has been filtered through English journals and a 1773 book. We cannot hear the conversation they had with each other, which Banks said they seemed to hold “earnestly.”

Why might they say it? If we stay with what the visitors wrote, the two men were telling the boats to stay off, and they did not give way until after shots were fired. Later Gweagal commentary, including Dr Williams’s, adds that people on the shore already knew the ship was coming, and that “Warra warra wai” can be heard as a reading of the strangers, not only as a challenge. That later knowledge is evidence too, and it is not the same thing as a 1770 transcript.

My finding

The three witness cards are James Cook, Joseph Banks, and the Gweagal men at Kamay. Further questions — including the shield and spears — are in Super Sleuths. There is no answer at the back.

His secret instructions said to take possession “with the Consent of the Natives.” Did he have it?

Write a finding. You may use one of these, or another wording of your own: The journals do not show consent, and they show opposition · He thought, for a moment, that gifts had been taken as a welcome — then wrote that he was mistaken · Formal possession was later, at Possession Island, still without a recorded consent · We can’t know, from this file, the exact words the two men would have used for their own law of entry · Another finding.

Why it still matters

The word “consent” is in the 1768 instructions. The 1770 journals are still the main written record of the first hours at Kamay. Whether those hours, or the later flag-raising in the Torres Strait, could count as consent is not only a history-class puzzle. It sits under later arguments about sovereignty, about Country, and about objects taken from the beach — spears still in Cambridge, and a shield in the British Museum whose link to this landing is now disputed. A careful finding will say what the journals show, and what they cannot say for the Gweagal men themselves.

Timeline

  • Late April 1770 — Endeavour enters Kamay. Cook’s landing journal: 29 April. Banks’s landing journal: 28 April.

  • 6 May 1770 — The ship leaves the bay (after eight days, in the usual count).

Witness files (seven headings)

James Cook

Background. A Royal Navy lieutenant, already experienced as a surveyor, given secret instructions about possession.

How he came to the place. West from New Zealand, then north along the coast, into the bay he would later call Botany Bay.

Movements before the incident. He saw people and huts as the ship came in, and went in the boats “in hopes of speaking with them.”

The incident. The opposed landing, the shots, the darts, the going up to the houses.

After the incident. Eight days in the bay; then, months later, the ceremony at Possession Island.

Evidence held. His journal (SLNSW Safe 1/71 and published transcriptions).

Not asked. He is not asked here to speak for Tupaia, or for the men on the rock.

Joseph Banks

Background. A wealthy young naturalist, keeping his own journal.

How he came to the place. As a civilian on Cook’s ship, in the same boats.

Movements before the incident. He had already watched people through glasses as the ship came in, and described two men painted in white, “brandishing their crooked weapons… as in token of defiance.”

The incident. The parley, the shots, the shield, the thrown lances, the taking of forty or fifty lances.

After the incident. Collecting plants; later fame as Sir Joseph Banks. The spears’ later history is below.

Evidence held. His journal, SLNSW, and the Wikisource / published text.

Not asked. Parkinson’s 1773 book is a fourth European record, used on the Gweagal card, not as a fourth front witness.

The Gweagal men at Kamay

Background. Gweagal people of the Dharawal-speaking area around the southern shore of Kamay. Later knowledge holders, including Dr Shayne Williams, say people already knew the ship was coming up the coast.

How they came to the place. They were already there. The visitors arrived.

Movements before the incident. Cook and Banks describe people on the points of the bay, then two men coming down as the boats closed.

The incident. Opposition to the landing, as recorded in the journals.

After the incident. The visitors remained eight days. Spears were taken. A shield may have been taken; see the objects debate.

Evidence held. Only through other people’s writing in 1770–73, plus later oral history, which does not all agree on the name Cooman.

Not asked. This file does not invent their speech, and it does not treat one descendant campaign as the only family view.

The Gweagal shield and the Kamay spears

What is on the record. Banks wrote that the elder man went back for a “sheild,” and that the landing party took “all the lances which we could find about the houses, amounting in number to forty or fifty.” Some of those spears later passed, through Cook and the Earl of Sandwich, to Trinity College, Cambridge, and are now at the Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, Cambridge. There has been a public campaign, including by Rodney Kelly, for the return of spears and of a shield held by the British Museum. Some spears have been lent or, in later announcements, agreed for return; students should check the current museum statements rather than treat this paragraph as the last word on custody.

The British Museum shield. An Aboriginal shield in the British Museum was long presented as the shield from this landing. Research published around 2016–18 (including work discussed by Nicholas Thomas, and by Maria Nugent and Gaye Sculthorpe) found that the Museum’s shield is made of red mangrove, a wood not available at Botany Bay; that a hole in it is unlikely to have been made by a firearm; and that it does not closely match an early drawing of a shield collected on the voyage. On that evidence, many specialists now think the Museum shield is probably not the 1770 Kamay shield. Shayne Williams has suggested it is more likely a shield from further north. The campaign for return, and the importance of a real 1770 shield if it still exists, are not cancelled by that doubt. The conflict belongs on this page, un-resolved.

Cooman. The name is not in Cook or Banks. It appears in later oral history and in some public campaigns. Other local Aboriginal voices have said there are other, more feasible candidates in family histories, or that there is no consensus. Do not write “Cooman’s shield” as a proved 1770 caption.

What we still can’t know
  • The two men’s names, on the 1770 evidence. Cooman is a later, disputed identification.
  • A full translation, made that day, of what they called out — except Parkinson’s later-published “Warra warra wai,” whose meaning is explained later.
  • Whether the British Museum shield is the shield from this landing. Current specialist research says it is probably not.
  • Whether Cook understood “consent,” in the instructions, as something that had to be clearly given, or as something he could infer. He left no separate essay on that word.
Sure / maybe / guess

Sure, from this file. The 1768 wording about consent. The opposed landing. Musket fire and a man struck by small shot. Darts or lances thrown. Spears taken. Cook’s line that they seemed to want the visitors gone. No recorded consent in the Kamay journals.

Maybe. The exact civil date (28 or 29 April). Whether a particular surviving shield is the 1770 one. How to hear “Warra warra wai.” Whether one of the men was Cooman.

Guess — do not write these as fact. Invented dialogue for the two men. That Cook “must have known” he had consent because he later raised a flag. That every Gweagal family today tells the landing the same way.
